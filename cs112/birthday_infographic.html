The Birthday
Problem
How many people do you need in a room before two share a birthday? The answer is far lower than intuition suggests — and it explains why hash collisions are inevitable.
23
people are enough for a >50% chance
of a shared birthday Out of 365 possible days — only 6.3% of slots needed
How the math works
1
Flip the question
Instead of asking "will someone share my birthday?", ask: what's the chance everyone has a different birthday? Then subtract from 1.
2
Add people one at a time
Each new person must land on one of the remaining free days. Person 1: 365/365. Person 2: 364/365. Person 3: 363/365. And so on…
3
Multiply the probabilities
P(all distinct) = 365/365 × 364/365 × 363/365 × … × 343/365
= ∏ (1 − i/365) for i = 0…22 ≈ 0.4927
P(collision) = 1 − 0.4927 = 0.5073 ≈ 50.7%

Why this matters for hash tables
⚡
Replace 365 days → M table slots. Replace 23 people → k inserted keys.

The math is identical. With a table of M = 365 slots, you only need k = 23 keys — just 6.3% full — before there's a 50% chance of collision. Real hash tables use much smaller M relative to k, so collisions are not edge cases. They are guaranteed. This is why every hash table must handle collisions — not as an afterthought, but as a core design requirement.
