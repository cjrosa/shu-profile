Advanced Learning Settings
How are Q-values updated?

After each move, the robot slightly revises its estimate of how useful that action was. It considers both the immediate reward and the best future reward it currently expects from the next state.

Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α[r + γ max Q(s′,a′) − Q(s,a)]
Run one move to see an example with the robot’s actual values.

Try an Experiment

Make a prediction before applying a scenario. The explanation stays open-ended until you observe the results.

1. No Cost for Wandering

Prediction: With no movement cost, will the robot still prefer a short path?

Apply Scenario removes the movement cost and resets learning. Then train 100 episodes to compare route efficiency in Policy view.

Observe: Does the learned route value efficiency?

Explain: How did removing a consequence for extra moves affect behavior?

2. Exploration Off

Prediction: What might happen if the robot never tries unfamiliar actions?

Apply Scenario disables exploration and resets learning. Then train 100 episodes to inspect how early choices limit the policy.

Observe: Did its early choices limit what it discovered?

Explain: Why can exploration be necessary?

3. Risky Shortcut

Prediction: Will the robot favor the short route beside danger or a longer safe path?

Apply Scenario configures and resets the complete environment. Then train 100 episodes to reveal the learned policy around the hazard.

Observe: Where does the policy turn away from risk?

Explain: How do nearby rewards influence earlier actions?

4. Misleading Reward

Prediction: What will happen if another square pays more than the intended goal?

Apply Scenario adds the larger competing reward and resets learning. Then train 100 episodes to see which terminal reward the policy pursues.

Observe: Did the specified reward produce the intended behavior?

Explain: The system follows its objective. What was misspecified?

5. Value of the Future

Prediction: How will valuing later rewards less change the policy?

Apply either discount setting to reset learning, then train 100 episodes. The comparison opens Q-values at the starting cell to show how far goal information propagated.

Observe: How far from the goal do useful action values appear?

Explain: How did delayed reward influence earlier decisions?

What this model can—and cannot—show

The grid makes agent, state, action, and reward easy to see. Q-learning stores a useful-action estimate for every state–action pair and improves those estimates through trial and error.

But success here does not mean the system understands navigation. This world has only 36 squares, four actions, exact rewards, and fixed rules. Real environments can be ambiguous, changing, and far larger. A learned policy reflects the environment and objective it was trained on.

Concept check

Agent: the robot · Environment: the grid · State: its current square · Action: one move · Reward: a numerical consequence · Episode: one attempt from start to finish · Policy: its current action choice for each state.
